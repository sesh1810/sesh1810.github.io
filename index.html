Shipping Agentic AI Inside Enterprise Middleware

How we brought autonomous AI agents into Oracle Integration Cloud — and what I'd do differently as a TPM

What Actually Happened

I was a Senior Member of Technical Staff on Oracle Integration Cloud (OIC). In 2024, our team took on a big bet: embedding agentic AI capabilities directly into OIC3, Oracle's middleware platform that thousands of enterprises use to connect their SaaS apps, databases, and on-prem systems.

The idea was straightforward. Instead of customers hand-wiring every integration flow, an AI agent could understand a business request in natural language, reason about which connectors and APIs to call, execute a multi-step integration plan, and handle errors autonomously. We called it Agentic AI in OIC.

I built parts of this system as an engineer. Here's the TPM story I now see underneath it.

The Architecture We Shipped

→

The agent treats OIC's existing connectors and integration recipes as tools. It can discover what's available in a customer's environment, compose a multi-step flow, execute it, and adapt when something fails. The key constraint: every action the agent takes must be auditable and reversible. Enterprise customers expect full traceability on anything touching their production systems.

The Hard Parts (Where a TPM Was Needed)

As an engineer, I focused on making the agent work. Looking back, the organizational complexity dwarfed the technical problems.

The Real Challenge

Three teams owned pieces of this: the AI/ML team building the agent reasoning layer, the core platform team owning OIC's connector infrastructure, and the security team who had to sign off on an AI system making autonomous API calls on behalf of enterprise customers. Nobody had a shared definition of "done."

If I'd been the TPM on this program, here's what I would have driven:

Problem 1
No shared success criteria. The AI team measured agent accuracy. The platform team measured connector uptime. Security measured audit coverage. A TPM's first job: define a single metric everyone agrees on. I'd propose successful autonomous integration completions per day, end-to-end, with security audit passing.
Problem 2
The trust gap with enterprise customers. Enterprises don't want an AI autonomously calling their Salesforce API. The rollout needed a progressive trust model: start with the agent suggesting integration flows for human approval, then graduate to semi-autonomous execution with guardrails, then full autonomy for low-risk flows only. This sequencing is TPM work.
Problem 3
Connector compatibility wasn't binary. OIC has 100+ connectors. The agent could use maybe 40 of them reliably at launch. Somebody needed to own the connector certification matrix, prioritize which connectors to onboard next based on customer demand, and communicate clearly what's supported vs. what's coming. Engineers don't naturally do this. TPMs do.

What I'd Ship as a TPM

WorkstreamOwnerTPM Role
Agent reasoning + tool selection AI/ML Team Define accuracy thresholds per connector type; gate launches on pass rates
Connector certification for agent use Platform Team Prioritize certification queue by customer revenue impact; track coverage %
Security + audit trail for autonomous actions Security Team Co-author the agent permissions model; ensure every agent action is logged and reversible
Customer rollout + trust building Product + GTM Design the progressive autonomy ladder; run the beta program with 10 design-partner customers

What I Learned

  • Shipping AI in the enterprise is mostly alignment work. Getting security, platform, and AI teams to agree on what "safe enough" means took longer than building the agent itself
  • Enterprise customers need a trust ladder. Suggest first, assist second, act third. We learned this the hard way when early demos spooked a design partner by auto-triggering their SAP connector
NUS Singapore Research Program 4 min read

Coordinating Surgeons and ML Engineers

A biomedical AI research collaboration that was, in hindsight, a technical program management exercise

The Project

At the National University of Singapore, I worked on a research project to detect Intracerebral Hemorrhage (brain bleeds) from CT scans using deep learning. The work was published in Medical & Biological Engineering & Computing (Springer).

My title was Research Collaborator. But the actual job? Sitting between neurosurgeons who needed clinical accuracy and ML engineers who needed clean data and clear labels. That's coordination work. That's TPM work.

What Made This Hard

The surgeons and the engineers spoke completely different languages. Different success criteria, different timelines, different definitions of "accurate." Every meeting needed a translator.

Gap 1
What counts as "accurate"? The ML team optimized for AUC-ROC. The surgeons cared about sensitivity (don't miss a bleed) over specificity (false alarms are okay, missed bleeds kill people). I had to translate the clinical priority into a loss function the engineers could implement.
Gap 2
Annotation bottleneck. Radiologists are expensive and busy. We needed thousands of labeled CT scans, but our radiologist collaborators could annotate maybe 50 per week. I had to negotiate a labeling protocol that was fast enough for the engineers but rigorous enough for the clinicians. We settled on a two-tier system: coarse labels for training, expert-reviewed labels for validation.
Gap 3
Publication vs. product timelines. The surgeons wanted a published paper with clinical validation. The engineering team wanted to ship a working model. These are different deliverables with different quality bars. I managed both tracks in parallel, with shared milestones where the work overlapped.
The TPM Realization

I didn't know the term "TPM" during this project. But I was doing the job: aligning stakeholders who don't share a vocabulary, managing dependencies between teams with different incentives, and making sure the final output met everyone's definition of success. The published paper was the deliverable. Getting surgeons and engineers to agree on what goes in it -- that was the actual program.

What I Learned

  • Vocabulary kills more cross-domain projects than technology does. Our model worked within weeks. Getting agreement on what "works" means in clinical terms took months
  • The most valuable person on the team was the one who could translate between domains. I spent more time in whiteboards with surgeons than in Jupyter notebooks, and that's where the project actually moved forward
